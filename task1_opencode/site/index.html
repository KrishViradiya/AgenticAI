01 / THE SYSTEMS PERSON

Nirav

M.Sc. Computer Science at Paderborn University; B.E. in Computer Engineering.

Into: Backend systems, table tennis, and trying every street food stall at a new place.

Fun fact

I have named every one of my laptops after a Bollywood villain.

AI, today

AI is a fast junior developer that never gets tired but also never says "I'm not sure". It saves me hours on boilerplate, but I still read every line before it goes anywhere near main.

In 5–10 years

Writing code will be the easy part. The hard part will be designing systems, defining what "correct" means and keeping agents from breaking each other's work.

Hot takeAn AI that writes 1,000 lines a minute just means 1,000 lines a minute someone has to review.

AI, today

AI can produce a working UI in minutes, but "working" and "pleasant to use" are not the same thing. It still needs a human eye for design, accessibility and the small details users notice.

Building software will feel more like directing than typing. Taste, user empathy and clear communication will matter more than knowing a framework.

AI, today

AI is great for scripts, configs and debugging error messages, but it happily suggests insecure shortcuts. Security and operations still need a human who understands the risks.

Agents will deploy, monitor and fix systems on their own, so the real job will be setting the guardrails: permissions, tests and rollback plans.

05 / THE ML SIDE-QUESTER

Harsh

M.Sc. Computer Science at Paderborn University; B.E. in Computer Engineering.

Into: Machine learning, football, and cooking dishes my mother would not approve of.

Fun fact

I have more half-finished ML side projects than finished meals this semester.

AI, today

The models are surprisingly good at explaining code and writing tests, but they are weak at understanding why a system was built the way it was. They work best when the human brings the context.

In 5–10 years

Every developer will manage a small team of agents. Good engineers will be the ones who can break a problem into clear tasks and verify the results quickly.

Hot takeMost "AI bugs" are just human requirements that were never written down.

The team's shared view

AI can ship the draft.
Humans own the direction.

AI agents are already good teammates for boilerplate, tests and first drafts, but they still need humans who understand the system, set the direction and check the result.

We expect the job to shift from writing code to specifying, reviewing and verifying it. The engineers who do well will be the ones who can explain clearly what they want and notice quickly when the answer is wrong.
